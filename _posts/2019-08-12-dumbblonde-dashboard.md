Have you ever been asked to build a live dashboard that looks like a slick sales pitch slide? You spend hours picking the right hue for the bar chart. Then someone asks you to turn the bars into curvy lines, because that looks more like Google Analytics...

Release day comes. Three weeks later the dashboard has, like, 2 views, 0 follow-up questions and 0 exports.

The good news: you don't have to maintain it anymore. The bad news: your existence as an Analyst is now in doubt, at least at that company. And the ugly news: the dashboard was useless from the start. It was made to make somebody feel important.

Gruesome, right?!

## So what makes a dashboard useful?

A dashboard is useful when it pushes the reader toward an action. Any action. Change a marketing practice, start selling a different product, and so on. If nobody can act on it, it's useless.

One caveat: don't mistake a good dashboard that only brings good news for a useless one. Think of a health check report where every disease box says negative. There's still an action there, and it's to keep the wheels rolling.

## How to build a non-dumb-blonde dashboard

Everything on a dashboard is an answer to a question someone asked while it was being built. So a good dashboard needs two things:

1. The right questions get asked.
2. The right answers show up on time.

Most interview questions for Analyst positions are about the second part. That's only half the job. [Asking the right questions](https://twitter.com/fchollet/status/837188765500071937) comes first, and it matters most.
