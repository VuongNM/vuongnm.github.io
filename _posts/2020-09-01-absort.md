Say you have a list of experiments to compare. Each one is a pair like (number_of_trials, number_of_successes). How do you sort them by performance?

The two obvious answers are both wrong.

## Sorting by successes minus failures

This lets popular experiments bubble up to the top. An experiment with 80 successes and 20 failures out of 100 trials scores 60. One with 8 successes out of 10 scores 6. The first wins by a mile, even though the second does better.

## Sorting by average rate

Sorting by n_success / n_trials ignores how much data you have. It puts 50 successes out of 100 on the same level as 1 success out of 2. The second one has a tiny sample, so any estimate from it is unreliable.

## Sort by the lower bound instead

The fix is to sort by the lower bound of a confidence interval on the success rate. The question becomes: given this data, what value is the true rate above, with 95% probability?

Using the lower bound instead of the average stops you from overestimating things you have little data on. If you don't have much data on something, you shouldn't be very optimistic about it.

![image](/images/absort.png )
<center><em>Same average rate (70%), different amounts of data: 7 out of 10 (blue) and 70 out of 100 (orange).</em></center>

The chart shows why. Both experiments have a 70% success rate. With only 10 trials, the blue curve is wide, and its 95% lower bound sits around 0.39. With 100 trials, the orange curve is narrow, and its lower bound is around 0.61. Sorting by the lower bound puts the experiment you can actually trust first.

I took this [idea](https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-sort-by-average-rating.html) from Evan Miller, who uses the lower bound of the Wilson confidence interval, and improved the formula with a Jeffreys prior to make the Bayesian fans happy.
