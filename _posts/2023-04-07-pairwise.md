title: Why local search struggles in high dimensional spaces
excerpt: In high dimensions, every point sits about the same distance from every other point, so "nearby" stops meaning much.

In the [previous post](/typical-sets/), I showed why starting an MCMC chain at the MAP is a bad idea. High dimensional space doesn't behave the way our low dimensional intuition says it should. Most points of a Gaussian don't sit near the center. They sit in a thin shell called the Typical Set.

This post looks at another side effect of that shell. In high dimensions, every point ends up about the same distance from every other point, and that hurts any method that searches locally, like nearest neighbors.

## The experiment

Draw points from a standard Gaussian in d dimensions and measure the distance between every pair. Here are those pairwise distances, for d from 2 to 902:

![image](/images/pair.png )
<center><em>Pairwise distances between points of a standard Gaussian, from 2 to 902 dimensions.</em></center>

Two things happen as d grows. The whole distribution moves right: the typical distance is about sqrt(2d), so around 14 at d = 102 and around 42 at d = 902. And the width of each bump barely changes.

So at d = 902, almost every pair of points is somewhere between 38 and 47 apart. Your closest pair and your farthest pair differ by only about 20%. Compared to how far apart everything is, all the points are roughly the same distance from each other.

## Why it happens

The math behind it is this limit:

![image](/images/pairwise_dist_limit.png )

As d grows, two random vectors become almost perpendicular, so the distance between them is just Pythagoras: sqrt(\|\|x\|\|² + \|\|y\|\|²). For a Gaussian, every \|\|x\|\| is close to sqrt(d) (that's the shell), so every distance is close to sqrt(2d). If you put the points on a unit hypersphere instead, every pairwise distance goes to sqrt(2).

Push d to infinity and the ratio between the distance to your nearest neighbor and the distance to your farthest point goes to 1. [This answer](https://stats.stackexchange.com/questions/29627/euclidean-distance-is-usually-not-good-for-sparse-data-and-more-general-case) goes deeper if you're curious.

## What it means for local search

Local search bets that "near" means something: if a point is good, its neighbors are probably good too. When every point is about as far from you as every other point, your nearest neighbor is barely nearer than a random one. The search loses its sense of direction and does little better than random search.

You'll see this when you have a moderate number of points and a lot of dimensions, which is another way of saying sparse data. Text vectors are the classic example.
