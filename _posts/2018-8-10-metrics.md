title: All metrics are wrong, but some are useful

excerpt: A metric squeezes a whole system into one number. Useful, as long as you remember what it leaves out.
---

George Box famously said:

    "All models are wrong, but some are useful".

He meant statistical models, but it holds for every scientific model. My point in this post: the same is true for metrics.

## Models are wrong on purpose

A model describes a system or a natural process, but only approximately. Box's warning was to never forget that part.

A statistical model is a set of equations describing the process that generated the data you observe. Some models describe the data more accurately than others, but none of them is the process itself.

A map is a good everyday example. It's wrong about the world (it has no height and no depth), yet it's exactly what you need to find your way around.

## Metrics are wrong in the same way

A metric is a number computed from the properties of a system. You use it to rate how well a process is working, or to compare one system to another. Metrics are usually designed to be monotonic (higher is better, or lower is better), so they are easy to read.

The catch is that a metric tries to squeeze everything you know about how well a system works into a single number. That's a lossy process, and it always gives you a biased view.

A few examples:

- **Accuracy hides rare cases.** Say a disease shows up in 1% of people. A model that always predicts NO gets 99% accuracy. It's useless (and reckless), and accuracy alone won't tell you that, because a model optimized for accuracy happily ignores recall.
- **F1 ignores the cost of being wrong.** F1 is fine when a false negative and a false positive cost about the same. When they don't, F1 is blind to the difference, and you should build the cost of each kind of mistake into the metric yourself.
- **One number hides the spread.** Describing a group with a single mean, median or mode throws away its variance.

## But metrics are useful

A metric never describes a system perfectly, but often it's all you have. Your analysis, your charts and even your head can only hold so much. You can stare at distribution plots, but boiling them down to something you can act on is still hard, and that's what a metric does for you.

So design your metric for its reader. A metric for a machine should be precise and have good numerical properties. A metric for humans should be clear and intuitive. And whoever uses it should know its strengths and its blind spots, so they don't get fooled by it.
