You launch a feature, and your metric doesn't jump overnight. It ramps up over a few weeks, like the ramen chart above. How do you measure an effect like that? In this post I model it with a sigmoid inside a Bayesian model.

## Where the classic tools fall short

Say you track a stable metric and just rolled out a killer feature. You want to know how much it moves the metric over time. Usually you'd run an A/B test.

But an A/B test assumes the metric stays flat over time. If it's already drifting, say with a linear trend, the test will mislead you.

So you run a regression of some kind and control for time. (Time is really a stand-in for whatever is actually driving the drift, but that's okay.)

The classic tool here is [diff-in-diff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_in_differences). In short, you add a binary variable that switches on when the change happens. That works for a sudden jump. It can't describe a change that builds up gradually.

## A sigmoid describes a gradual change

Sigmoids are fun. They're smooth and have nice analytical gradients. People mostly know them from classification, but they're also a great shape for a change that starts slowly, speeds up, then levels off.

In this [notebook](https://github.com/VuongNM/study/blob/main/sigmoid%20surge.ipynb), I add a sigmoid to a regression to model exactly that. The sigmoid has three parameters, and each one answers a question about the effect:

 - **Location:** when did the change happen?
 - **Scale:** how big is it? (Remember, it doesn't have to top out at 1.)
 - **Slope:** was it sudden or gradual?

Once the model is fit, these parameters tell you about the effect in your data.

Because sigmoids have nice gradients, you could bolt one onto a classical linear regression and fit it with gradient descent. But I'm not so classical, am I?

## Enter Bayes, with his big gun PyMC3

PyMC3 lets you write the model clearly and flexibly. You write down a function with parameters *θ* and fit it to your data. Instead of a single best estimate of *θ*, you get the whole distribution of *θ*.

That distribution is the point. It tells you how uncertain each estimate is: maybe you're sure the change happened in week 5, but not sure how big it was. You can make better decisions with that in hand.

So I take a simple linear regression, add a sigmoid on top, and let PyMC3 work its magic and find the distribution of the parameters.

More than one effect? Just use more sigmoids ;)
