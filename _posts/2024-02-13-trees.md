Trees, random forests and boosted trees are everywhere, and most people tune them by poking at parameters until the score goes up. In this post I go from where trees come from to how I'd actually tune a boosted tree, and the sins to avoid.

## The seed of the trees

Trees came about when people realized that writing rules into machines by hand takes toooo much work (Prolog, anyone?). Wouldn't it be nice if the machine could work out the rules itself, from a bunch of examples? That's what a decision tree does.

## Trees have high variance, and random forests fix that

Trees are notorious for high variance. On a complex problem with many predictors, they get unwieldy and their output jumps around. People don't like that. They want their trees to have a certain shape, so they prune them. Pruning adds some bias to the tree on purpose, to make it behave in a way you want. Keep this idea in mind, we'll come back to it.

Tree lovers also found that if you build a bunch of trees and average them, the predictions get much more stable. That's a random forest. A random forest is essentially variance reduction.

## Boosted trees

Boosting, as an old master put it, is one of the finest ideas to come out of the field. It has deep roots (pun!) in mathematics, stemming (another pun) from Newton and Leibniz's integrals: slice a domain thin enough and you can add up the slices to get the whole. Boosting does the same. In theory, you can add up many thin "slice" estimators, one after another, to model a function very accurately. (In practice, real data has noise and measurement errors, so you have to know when to stop. More on that later.)

Boosted trees beat other kinds of trees nowadays, and it makes sense. Modern implementations like xgboost are worth the best money, and their creators gave them away for free. If everyone who ever made a profit or saved a dime with xgboost paid them a little, they'd be so fucking rich.

Boosting is basically summation. You can't go wrong letting a computer do sums, can you? Yet I've seen people come in with their trees, tune them upside down, then fuck off claiming it couldn't be done.

## How to tune a boosted tree

**First, know the god damn trees.** Really learn them. My best advice: learn from the old masters, and from more than one. Learn from the people who created the algorithm and the people who turned it into code. Drink from the source. You only have to learn it once in your life, and if you work in this field, sooner or later you'll need to fit a tree. This is a must!

**Second, know what you can tune and how.** That depends on the implementation. For xgboost and its cousins, go look at [this parameter guide](https://sites.google.com/view/lauraepp/parameters). It's your map.

**Third, apply your theory.** Usually you set the learning rate first. Smaller is better, but slower, so pick your balance. The next most important knob is the depth of each tree:

- Too shallow, and each tree learns very little, so you need a lot of them.
- Too deep, and each tree is complex with high variance. Remember, boosting is a bias reduction technique. Big, complicated trees dig too far into the details, overshoot, and the following trees have to correct for it.

That's why xgboost can give you negative predictions when all your targets are non-negative. A single tree can't predict outside the range it has seen. The negative values come from the trees correcting each other.

## Sins. May you burn in hell if you commit them

- **Tuning the god damn random seed.** I've seen this many times. It's for Kaggle fuckwads and crooks. Please god, wash my eyes with your holy water.
- **Random searching the whole parameter space**, especially the important ones like learning rate and tree depth. You might land on a combination that is dangerous, like a bunch of very deep trees (high variance, over-correcting each other) that just happen to minimize your CV score. And who needs you, if all you do is random search on the very thing you're supposed to bring value to? You'd be defeating your own purpose.
- **Listening to fuckwads like me.** Remember, first-hand knowledge beats second-hand.

## So what should you do?

It's hard, and it depends on your problem. Use the scientific method: start with a theory, then check it with your testing scheme. Random search is ok, but it's a very, very dangerous tool. One day your model will break, and if you got there by random search, you won't know why. This is about having principles. It's the science in data science. Know how to ride your bike, and know how to fix it.
