My claim in this post: between two models with the same error, pick the one you understand and control, even if it's more complicated. I'll make the case with a story, then with a bit of math.

## The blackbox treadmill

Model selection is hard. If all you do is treat your ML model as a blackbox, it's very hard. It's lazy thinking, and fucking dangerous. You're on a treadmill of never-ending horror. One day your model will fail, that's a certainty, and you won't know why.

When that day comes, you have three options. Pray the model is so unimportant that everyone, you included, lets it slide. Cook up a new blackbox model and hope it fixes things this time. Or lie. Tuning the random seed is accepted and upvoted on Kaggle these days. Gold medals and such, what lying are you talking about?

The blackbox mindset hides the way to the solution. If you instead see your model as what it is, a deterministic algorithm (data is code and code is data, more on that later), you know that each kind of problem has a fix, and you know how far your solution can take you.

## The story of Tony

I was working on a forecasting project with a guy named Tony, the head of merchandising. This chad is a human forecasting system running on carbohydrates, with years of experience.

He does everything in Excel. His Excel file is an ecosystem of its own, with birds and bugs. It's fucking special. He can smell a number and tell you right off his nose whether it's over or under forecast. I call that grandma wisdom (Taleb's words). He just knows, and he doesn't need any fAnCy formula to explain it. He was single-handedly holding the previous forecast engine together. The problem: there's only one Tony and a quarter million products, and there's only so much he can go through.

<center><em>Tony the Chad, bearer of grandma's wisdom.</em></center>

My forecast feeds into Tony's plans, so Tony is, rightfully, the final boss my forecast has to beat before release. He slices the forecast in some magical way, and wham! Weird shit shows up. You hate him for breaking the illusion of your mighty model, but you have to respect him, because he's almost always right.

So I keep changing the system. Oh, have I told you about the time limit? The forecast has to be ready within 3 hours: after the data syncs, and before Tony shows up on the 1st of each month.

What started as a simple boosted trees model became a monster ensemble that couldn't be trained on a single machine. Many parts of the original idea got replaced. By the end, bits of Tony's wisdom were baked into the model one way or another: in how the data was sliced and sub-sampled, and in how the model was evaluated.

Now, say you have 2 models with the same MAPE error, one with Tony's pill and one without. Which one would you choose, and why?

![image](/images/pills.jpg)

A scarier question: would a blackbox model, tuned by random parameter search, get past Tony the Chad? Probably not. Quite frankly, I'm scared to work that way.

Let me convince you with something more scientific.

## The Pythagoras equation of machine learning

Say you have a regression problem and pick an estimator. Its error splits like the Pythagoras theorem:

	`Mean Squared Error = Bias**2 + Variance`

(plus some noise you can't do anything about). So your error comes from two sources, bias and variance. Push one down and the other tends to go up. That trade-off is what model selection is about.

ML pop culture says: given the same error, pick the simpler model. But nobody explains what "simpler" means, so people take it as "pick a simpler algorithm". Then they throw a fit when the results get worse. Fuck off, I want my trees, right?

Simpler models do tend to have more bias. Not always, but mostly. The reverse isn't true, though: a more biased model isn't always simpler. The model I built for Tony is sure as hell not simpler, but it's definitely more biased. I bent it to behave in certain, predictable ways. I put biases in on purpose.

Another example: in a Bayesian model, you can add bias through a hierarchical structure, or by putting constraints on the parameters. The ML literature understands constraints well, but structure much less, let alone uses it. With good structure, you can even afford a model with more parameters than training samples.

## Prefer the bias you put there

So among models that perform about the same, favor the one with more bias, especially bias that comes from structure, because that's bias you can steer. You can put it in through the algorithm you choose, the constraints on the parameters, or the way you train and select the model. That bias is your control over the model. It's Tony's grandma wisdom.

A model where you know what it's doing, and how well, beats a slightly better blackbox. Use that as your Occam's razor when you select models.
